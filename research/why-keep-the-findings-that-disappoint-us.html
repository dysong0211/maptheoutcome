Why keep the findings that disappoint us?

An uncomfortable result can be the most useful part of the record.

MMap the OutcomeEditorial desk · Updated 9 September 2026

Key takeaway

  • Learning requires space for results that challenge the plan.
  • The original context matters when interpreting disappointment.
  • Contradictory evidence should inform the next investigation.

A programme archive should have room for a promising approach that did not deliver, an expected result that remained uncertain and a change that the team decided to reverse.

Franco, Malhotra and Simonovits examined 221 social-science experiments and found that strong results were 40 percentage points more likely to be published than null results. Their sample concerned survey experiments with American adults, not international aid programmes. It demonstrates a problem of selective visibility in research, rather than an estimate of hidden findings in development work. Science, 2014

The question I take from that study is what a programme record chooses to retain. If the archive is built around material suitable for public promotion, it may not serve the same purpose as a record intended to guide future decisions.

Imagine a team testing a new recruitment approach and seeing little change. The experience could still help the next team: which group was targeted, what was attempted, how long the test ran and how confidently the result can be interpreted.

An inconclusive observation should also stay inconclusive. It should not be relabelled as proof that the intervention failed merely to produce a neat lesson.

I would want MatOc to make these distinctions easy to preserve. Reviewers should be able to keep a record because it matters to the programme's understanding, even when it does not strengthen a success narrative.

The goal is a history that remains useful when the next decision is difficult. Disappointing and uncertain findings deserve a place in that history.
