Outcomes stop being the afterthought.

Map the Outcome (MtO) is real-time, AI-powered monitoring and evaluation software for development organizations, donors, UN agencies, and government programs. Most programs document outcomes only once, at the end, reconstructed from memory after the program has finished and the people who lived it have moved on. MtO changes that. Teams paste in what they're already writing — weekly updates, meeting notes, field reports — and AI extracts outcomes, risks, decisions, and open questions as they happen. The result is a living, evidence-ready record built in real time, so evaluations, donor reporting, and cross-program learning start from a real archive instead of a blank page. That means final reporting and evaluations start from the beginning in real time instead of six weeks of reconstruction — faster, cheaper, and closer to what actually happened and the lessons learned along the way.

Why this gap exists

it's not that teams don't care

Development projects spend their energy on the work itself while it's running. Documentation happens at the end, in the scramble before close, by whoever is still around — which the Gavi/Cambodia record above shows plainly: full financial detail, and nothing on outcomes, from a team that was clearly capable of thorough reporting when the standard asked for it.

An evaluator arrives and must reconstruct what happened from scratch — work the team already lived through once, in real time, when the outcomes occurred. The reconstruction is expensive and incomplete: the people who knew why decisions changed may have moved on by then, and the context goes with them.

The result: 5.7% outcome reporting is not a failure of evaluation. It's a failure of capture timing.

How real-time impact tracking works

Four steps: you share what you're doing. We read it. You approve what we understood. It becomes comprehensive evidence—not at close, but as work happens.

Step 1: You share

your project data

Every week you have material: meeting notes, field reports, community feedback, risk updates, a message thread someone sent at 9pm. Paste it into the notepad and say which outcome it belongs to. Rough and unstructured is fine—sorting it is the job. Nothing happens without your explicit action.

Paste any text, or attach a .txt or .md file. Your draft saves as you type, so nothing is lost if you close the tab.

Step 2: We extract

our AI reads it

Map the Outcome reads what you pasted. It pulls out what matters:

  • Progress: Beneficiaries reached, clinics opened, trainings completed
  • Risks: Budget cuts, staffing gaps, access problems, supply delays
  • Decisions: Why you changed approach, evidence at the time, what pivoted
  • Feedback: What communities said, what beneficiaries asked for, adaptation made
  • Methodology: How you measured, who you included, what changed

Step 3: You approve

author controls the record

Map the Outcome shows you exactly what it extracted, field by field, with a confidence score telling you how certain it is. Approve it or throw it out. Only approved items file, and nothing is ever filed on your behalf. You own the narrative.

Step 4: We archive

dated and attributed

Approved items are filed with full attribution—who added it and when—and every risk and open question stays linked to the note it came from, so you can always trace a claim back to its source. Your record is inspectable from day one.

About Map the Outcome

Why outcomes are underdocumented, and what we're doing about it.

The problem

outcomes are reconstructed, not recorded

Development programmes document their finances in real time and their outcomes almost never. A five-country IATI audit (4,368 health-sector activities, 2018–2025) shows funders and implementers named on roughly 95% and 89% of activities. A deeper, activity-level inspection of Cambodia's health-sector activities — 914 activities, 636 with full activity XML checked — found only 5.7% report an actual outcome result.

Gavi's pneumococcal vaccine programme in Cambodia makes the point on its own: $41.1 million committed, $40.7 million disbursed, 77 transactions, 96 budget periods — every dollar traceable. Zero outcome indicators recorded. Not because anyone withheld them — the reporting standard never asked for them. If a partnership this well-documented ships impact data without a narrative, the gap isn't an individual failure. It's structural.

Why this happens

not laziness, timing

Teams spend their attention on delivering the programme while it's running. Writing down what happened competes with doing what happened, and doing wins — until close, when the reasoning has to be reconstructed instead of recorded: who decided what, and why, months after the fact, often by people who weren't there for the decision.

The solution

capture as the work happens

Map the Outcome inverts the timeline. Instead of asking "what happened?" once, at the end, it captures the answer continuously — from what teams are already writing: weekly updates, meeting notes, field reports. Extracted, reviewed by a person, approved, filed. As the work happens, not after it.

What that changes, if it holds up through pilot: teams see their own story as it develops instead of losing it. Evaluators open a record instead of reconstructing one. The next programme starts with what the last one learned, instead of from zero.

Built on research standards

ALCOA from clinical trials

Map the Outcome is built on ALCOA principles from pharmaceutical research:

  • Attributable: Every entry shows who added it
  • Legible: No abbreviations, no jargon, readable to anyone
  • Contemporaneous: Recorded when it happened, not later
  • Original: Source documents linked
  • Accurate: Verified by the author before filing

This makes the record inspection-ready at any moment—not just at close. It's designed for auditing, evaluation, and learning.

No surveillance. You control.

consent first

Map the Outcome doesn't monitor teams. It doesn't parse emails. It doesn't scrape data from systems. It only reads what you explicitly share, and only after you approve what we understood.

You own the narrative. Nothing reaches your record without a person approving it first, and anything you reject is discarded rather than filed quietly.
