The final report should not be the first time a programme explains itself

Finishing a report should leave an organisation better able to explain its work. A useful test is what the next reader can establish: what changed, which evidence supports that conclusion, and what remains...

programme memorymonitoring and evaluationdonor reporting

Finishing a report should leave an organisation better able to explain its work. A useful test is what the next reader can establish: what changed, which evidence supports that conclusion, and what remains uncertain.

Public research suggests those questions can remain difficult. The 2024 Aid Transparency Index found that only about a third of its 50 assessed organisations published good, current results. Separately, the UN's 2025 inspection found widespread capacity pressures from donor reporting and oversight requests. These studies examine different populations and measures; together, they suggest a problem worth investigating between reporting effort and usable evidence. Aid Transparency Index, UN JIU review

Consider an illustrative programme with a complete quarterly report, an indicator spreadsheet and a folder of meeting notes. The report says delivery improved. The spreadsheet shows attendance. The meeting notes contain the explanation for a change in approach. Each document may serve its original purpose, while the connection between them still requires someone to reconstruct it.

That connection is central to Map the Outcome's proposed role. MtO is being built to associate reviewed evidence with intended outcomes, decisions and original sources as implementation continues.

A useful record should also retain complications: disagreement, incomplete coverage and results that did not follow the plan. Otherwise, a more polished account could simply make uncertainty harder to see.

The practical question for a pilot is whether someone unfamiliar with the programme can find and assess the evidence more easily. A completed report is an output of the workflow. A usable programme history is the capability MtO needs to demonstrate.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 02

Why development teams keep rebuilding the same evidence

Imagine a programme team answering the same underlying question for a donor update, a management review, a partner meeting and an evaluation request. The audiences differ, but the source material overlaps. The...

monitoring and evaluationdonor reportingoutcome evidence

Imagine a programme team answering the same underlying question for a donor update, a management review, a partner meeting and an evaluation request. The audiences differ, but the source material overlaps. The opportunity for improvement lies in how much of that work can be reused safely.

The scale of reporting is documented. A UN inspection recorded 3,000 WHO donor reports for 2016. That is a historical count, not a judgement that all those reports were unnecessary. A later harmonisation pilot found mixed results: some feedback indicated time savings, while other reports took the same time or longer. JIU, Annex II, GPPi's 2018 pilot review

The lesson for a product team is practical. A simpler template may help, but the work before the template also matters: finding the right source, checking its date, resolving conflicting versions and asking a colleague what a sentence meant.

MtO's proposed approach is to preserve reviewed evidence with its source and programme context, so subsequent requests can start from material already checked. The team would still decide which evidence answers the question and whether it remains current.

Reuse must also preserve boundaries. A record approved for one purpose may need updated context or different access permissions for another. Repeating a claim without checking its relevance would create a different kind of waste.

The measure of success should therefore be total effort per accepted response, including review and correction. MtO should earn its place by making that work easier while maintaining the quality of the answer.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 03

Delivery is not the same as outcome evidence

A programme can deliver its planned activities and still need to explain whether those activities changed anything meaningful. That explanation requires a record of what followed delivery, what else influenced...

donor reportingoutcome evidenceimplementation learning

A programme can deliver its planned activities and still need to explain whether those activities changed anything meaningful. That explanation requires a record of what followed delivery, what else influenced events and where the original assumptions held or failed.

MOPAN's 2026 assessment of UNDP illustrates this distinction: strong output-level performance coexisted with a corporate framework that could not systematically assess longer-term outcomes and impacts. Its WFP assessment also identified weaknesses in using monitoring for learning and adjustment. MOPAN, UNDP, MOPAN, WFP

For an illustrative maternal-health programme, the number of outreach sessions answers one question. Attendance answers another. Whether women received appropriate care, who remained excluded and why participation changed require further evidence.

MtO should help organise the pathway between these questions. A schedule change, stock shortage, community observation and attendance record can be linked without pretending they establish a complete causal explanation.

The distinction between a signal and a verified finding is essential. If a field note suggests that Saturday sessions improved attendance, the record should preserve that suggestion, identify its source and show what needs checking. An evaluator may later find supporting evidence, competing explanations or a different result.

This gives the programme team a more useful starting point during implementation as well as at evaluation. The question becomes specific: which part of the pathway needs investigation, and which evidence would help? MtO's value depends on whether it makes that inquiry easier.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 04

Programme memory should survive staff turnover

A useful final report should allow a new reader to understand both what was achieved and what remains unresolved. Programme memory includes the reasons behind decisions, the assumptions that changed and the...

outcome evidenceimplementation learningaid transparency

A useful final report should allow a new reader to understand both what was achieved and what remains unresolved. Programme memory includes the reasons behind decisions, the assumptions that changed and the responsibilities that continue after a grant ends.

The UN's knowledge-management review describes losses when expertise leaves and difficulties finding knowledge across fragmented repositories. Separately, GEF's portfolio assessment distinguishes satisfactory outcomes at completion from the likelihood that benefits will endure. Neither finding supports a claim that staff stop caring when programmes close. UN knowledge-management review, GEF portfolio assessment

My proposed standard for programme handover is simple: another person should be able to establish what the team tried, what changed, what evidence was available and what still needs attention.

That means retaining more than a list of achievements. A useful handover might explain why a delivery model changed, which partner will maintain a service, which risk remains open and when a promising result needs another check.

MtO is being built towards this kind of continuity. Its programme record should accumulate during implementation, with people reviewing the proposed evidence and preserving links to sources.

The system also needs an end-of-programme arrangement. Who can access the record after funding ends? Who can correct it? Which sensitive material should be restricted? What evidence must remain available independently of a subscription?

Those questions are part of preserving institutional memory. A record has lasting value when the next team can understand it, trust its provenance and use it responsibly.

Lessons learned should reach the next decision

“Engage stakeholders earlier” may be sensible advice. Without context, it gives the next programme little help deciding whom to involve, when to involve them or what problem the advice is meant to prevent. A useful...

implementation learningaid transparencyAI evidence systems

“Engage stakeholders earlier” may be sensible advice. Without context, it gives the next programme little help deciding whom to involve, when to involve them or what problem the advice is meant to prevent.

A useful lesson should carry enough information to be judged. What happened? Under which conditions? What did the team change? What evidence suggests the change helped? Where might the lesson fail to apply?

GEF's recent knowledge-management assessment identifies continuing weaknesses in capturing and sharing lessons despite formal improvements. MOPAN also found barriers to moving useful WFP knowledge across countries. These findings support attention to how knowledge reaches decisions, as well as how it is stored. GEF OPS8, knowledge management, MOPAN, WFP 2024

For MtO, this suggests a demanding definition of a lesson. A label attached to a paragraph is only the beginning. The record should connect the lesson to an intervention, a setting, an observed result and the source behind it.

A future network could then help a programme designer find relevant experience across programmes. Relevance would need to reflect meaningful conditions, including who participated, delivery constraints and differences between locations. Similar wording alone would be a weak basis for transferring advice.

This is a development ambition to test, not an established MtO capability. Early pilots should ask whether another team actually used a recorded lesson and whether it informed a concrete design choice.

Counting lessons stored would tell us how much material accumulated. Documenting how a lesson informed the next decision would tell us whether the learning system became useful.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 06

AI for evaluation needs source links, not magic

Consider this illustrative programme update: “Attendance seems to have improved after outreach moved to Saturdays. Community health workers said women were more available.” An AI system could turn that into a...

aid transparencyAI evidence systemsprogramme design

Consider this illustrative programme update: “Attendance seems to have improved after outreach moved to Saturdays. Community health workers said women were more available.”

An AI system could turn that into a confident sentence: “Saturday outreach increased attendance.” The wording is shorter, but the claim has changed. A tentative observation has become a causal conclusion.

MtO's review process should prevent that change from passing unnoticed. It should preserve the tentative result signal, identify who reported it, link the original passage and distinguish the schedule decision from the explanation proposed for its effect.

Human approval should mean that a reviewer has assessed what the source supports. It should not silently transform a field observation into an independently verified outcome. An extraction-confidence score would also need a precise meaning; it should not be presented as the probability that the programme caused the result.

These boundaries matter even in structured systems. IATI's result element can describe outputs, outcomes, impacts or other results, so the existence of a result record does not establish what kind of finding it contains. IATI result definition

Current expert discussion also asks how AI might change evaluation work. That is useful context, but commentary is not evidence that a particular product saves time or improves judgement. GPPi, May 2026 commentary

MtO should make claims easier to inspect: source, date, reviewer, uncertainty, competing evidence and revision history. Better access to that context would give evaluators and programme teams more material with which to exercise their own judgement.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 07

What portfolio learning needs before it can scale

An organisation may have reports for individual programmes and still struggle to answer a portfolio question: what changed across this body of work, for whom, and under which conditions? ICAI's sustainable cities...

AI evidence systemsprogramme designevaluation practice

An organisation may have reports for individual programmes and still struggle to answer a portfolio question: what changed across this body of work, for whom, and under which conditions?

ICAI's sustainable cities review encountered this problem. Inconsistent and insufficient results data prevented a quantitative analysis across the portfolio. The reviewers still identified positive achievements. Their in-depth programme sample was also explicitly not fully representative. ICAI, UK aid for sustainable cities

For MtO, the implication extends beyond document retrieval. A searchable collection can help someone find a claim, but aggregation requires agreement about what the claim measures.

Consider an illustrative portfolio in which one programme counts people attending a session, another counts repeat visits and a third reports households reached. Adding those numbers would produce an impressive total with no consistent meaning. The source links might all work while the comparison remained invalid.

A useful evidence layer should therefore retain definitions, units, time periods and relevant population boundaries. It should distinguish observations from estimates and record when the same event appears in several documents.

Narrative material needs similar care. A lesson about maintaining a service after a grant ends should remain connected to the institutions, resources and conditions that made maintenance possible.

MtO's programme network should grow from this discipline. The first objective is a reliable record for one programme. Connections across programmes become more useful as definitions, review practices and permissions become consistent.

The practical test is whether a portfolio reader can understand both the pattern and its limits without rebuilding the analysis from the beginning.

What an MtO pilot should prove

programme designevaluation practiceprogramme memory

A product can address a documented problem and still fail to save its users time. Setup may take longer than expected. AI suggestions may require extensive correction. An additional review queue may compete with the same work the product was meant to support.

MtO should test its value against those possibilities from the beginning.

The UN's 2025 donor-oversight review calls for better tracking of the costs organisations incur in responding to requests. That measurement gap is a reason to establish a baseline, rather than apply an assumed percentage saving to aid budgets. JIU/REP/2025/3, paragraph 50

A credible pilot would select real reporting and evidence-finding tasks, record how they are currently completed and compare the total effort with MtO. That total should include configuration, source preparation, review, correction and the final check that an answer is fit for its purpose.

Quality deserves equal attention. Can an independent reader trace a claim? Were important qualifications retained? Did the system miss evidence that contradicted the preferred account? Did another team understand the handover?

The pilot should also test whether use continues beyond a demonstration. A small number of carefully prepared examples will not reveal the effort required for an ordinary week of mixed documents and incomplete updates.

Any financial calculation should distinguish capacity released from expenditure reduced. Hours freed for field work may be valuable even when payroll costs remain unchanged.

For users and investors, the persuasive result would be documented improvement in a defined workflow, with the costs and limitations visible. That is the evidence MtO should build before making broad promises.

OECD's preliminary figures put 2025 ODA from DAC members and associates at US$174.3 billion, a 23.1% decline in real terms from 2024. These are preliminary funding statistics, not a measure of programme quality or wasted money. OECD, April 2026

The figures raise a practical question for programme leaders: when resources change, what evidence is available to decide what to continue, adapt, transfer or stop?

ICAI's 2026 review of UK ODA target management highlights problems associated with budget uncertainty and attention to spending targets over agreed results. Its findings concern a particular funding system, but illustrate why the relationship between money, decisions and outcomes deserves a visible record. ICAI, March 2026 review

For an illustrative programme facing a reduction, a useful record would show which activities changed, which assumptions no longer hold, what risks increased and what evidence supports the remaining approach. It should preserve the consequences of a decision as well as the decision itself.

MtO could support that record by linking approved updates and changes to intended outcomes. It would still leave resource allocation and programme judgement with the people responsible for them.

Product claims need restraint in this setting. Software cannot replace withdrawn services, specialist expertise or sustained funding. Its contribution must be demonstrated within the work it actually affects.

The proposition worth testing is whether clearer evidence helps teams use their available time more effectively and explain the consequences of programme choices. That is a concrete contribution against which MtO can be assessed.

MtO will enter an existing ecosystem of programme data and evaluation tools. Its purpose needs to be clear to an organisation that already has systems, trained staff and reporting obligations.

Public product descriptions show substantial capabilities already available. ActivityInfo supports programme data collection and reporting. DevResults supports monitoring and results management. MtO's proposed entry point is the documentation teams already produce during implementation. The product would propose outcome-relevant records, ask people to review them and retain their connections to sources, changes, risks and intended outcomes.

That focus needs to translate into a practical advantage. Can a team add a source once and reuse its reviewed evidence? Can an evaluator understand why an approach changed? Can a successor find the context behind an unresolved risk?

Integration is part of that test. MtO should respect existing permissions, preserve useful exports and work alongside established results frameworks. If it requires users to maintain another disconnected version of the programme, its proposed benefit becomes harder to realise.

The strongest positioning will come from demonstrated performance for a defined group of users. A pilot should establish which tasks MtO improves, what existing tools already do well and where a combined workflow is worthwhile. That would give the product a clearer foundation for adoption and future development.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 11

A programme should be allowed to change its mind

A programme plan is a set of commitments built around an explanation of how change might happen. I would want a team to take those commitments seriously while remaining willing to question the explanation. Imagine...

monitoring and evaluationdonor reportingoutcome evidence

A programme plan is a set of commitments built around an explanation of how change might happen. I would want a team to take those commitments seriously while remaining willing to question the explanation.

Imagine an outreach programme designed around weekday visits. Early feedback suggests the intended participants are unavailable during those hours. The team changes its schedule. The important questions are what prompted the decision, whether the change reached different people and what the evidence showed afterwards.

The proposal for Problem-Driven Iterative Adaptation developed by Andrews, Pritchett and Woolcock emphasises locally defined problems, experimentation and feedback. It offers an approach to institutional reform; it is not an experiment demonstrating that every programme benefits from constant adjustment. Andrews, Pritchett and Woolcock, 2012

My view is that a well-explained change should strengthen a programme's accountability. A reader should be able to see what the original plan expected, what the team encountered, which options it considered and why the revised approach was reasonable at the time.

That also leaves room to discover that a change did not help. A record built only around defending decisions would be a poor learning tool.

For MtO, this means preserving revisions and their reasons. An updated plan should remain connected to the earlier assumption and the evidence that challenged it. The record can then support a more useful conversation: did the team recognise the problem, respond appropriately and learn from what followed?

That is the kind of programme memory I would want to carry into the next funding cycle.

What a green dashboard leaves out

donor reportingoutcome evidenceimplementation learning

Imagine a programme dashboard with every activity marked green. Training happened on schedule. Materials were distributed. Spending followed the budget. A reader still needs to know whether the intended participants could use what was delivered and whether an important assumption has changed.

This is a question about what management asks people to pay attention to. Dan Honig's research argues that tight controls on field staff can undermine performance, particularly in unpredictable environments. The paper examines foreign aid implementation; its finding should not be turned into a claim that monitoring is generally harmful. Honig, International Organization, 2019

I would design a programme review to ask for both delivery information and consequential exceptions. Which concern needs a decision? Which result is less certain than it appears? Where has local judgement changed the approach?

A green status should leave those questions open. It should also be possible to report a problem without first turning it into a success story. Otherwise, the status label may tell a manager very little about the work that deserves attention.

The implication for MtO is practical: preserve observations that complicate the summary. A delivery milestone, a staff concern and a contradictory result can all belong in the same record, with their different meanings intact.

I would judge that record by whether it helps a reviewer ask a better next question. A concise dashboard is useful when the evidence beneath it remains accessible and the team can explain what the colour does not convey.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 13

Ask why implementation is struggling while there is time to respond

An explanation of implementation problems has particular value when someone can still act on it. The same observation may support a practical adjustment during delivery and a recommendation after the programme...

outcome evidenceimplementation learningaid transparency

An explanation of implementation problems has particular value when someone can still act on it. The same observation may support a practical adjustment during delivery and a recommendation after the programme ends.

A 2020 systematic review examined 34 studies using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research in 21 low- and middle-income countries. It found that 59% applied the framework at study endline, primarily for analysis or interpretation. That is a finding about the reviewed applications of one framework, not all programme evaluation. Systematic review, Implementation Science, 2020

The finding raises a useful design question: what would a team need to recognise an implementation barrier earlier? A framework alone does not answer it. Someone must notice the issue, make the observation available and have responsibility for deciding what to do.

Consider a hypothetical training programme where attendance is adequate but participants cannot use the new process because the required equipment is unavailable. Recording attendance remains useful. Recording the equipment constraint could support a different decision about procurement, sequencing or the training itself.

For MtO, earlier capture should mean keeping relevant observations available for review while implementation continues. It should not mean asking staff to complete an exhaustive diagnostic assessment every week.

A sensible pilot would select a few important barriers and examine whether the record helped the team recognise and respond to them. The test is not simply whether an explanation was documented sooner. It is whether earlier access made the explanation useful to someone with an opportunity to act.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 14

Local knowledge deserves a place in the evidence base

A published study can estimate what happened under particular conditions. A person working locally may understand a delivery constraint that the study never encountered. A useful programme record should make room...

implementation learningaid transparencyAI evidence systems

A published study can estimate what happened under particular conditions. A person working locally may understand a delivery constraint that the study never encountered. A useful programme record should make room to examine both contributions.

Vivalt, Coville and KC used a choice experiment with policy professionals attending World Bank and Inter-American Development Bank workshops. Participants placed substantial weight on local expert recommendations and locally relevant evidence. The choices were hypothetical: the experiment does not establish that local experts were correct or that those preferences improved programme outcomes. Author manuscript, 2024

For programme intelligence, I would want local advice to retain its attribution and reasoning. “The district team recommended a different delivery model” is a starting point. What constraint did they identify? What experience informed their judgement? Which parts of the recommendation can be checked?

Formal evidence deserves similar treatment. Where was it generated? Who implemented the intervention? How closely do the conditions match the programme being considered?

MtO should help people bring those questions together. It could preserve a local observation alongside a relevant study without automatically treating either as the final answer. Disagreement would remain visible, with an explanation of what additional information could resolve it.

The ambition is a record that respects knowledge enough to make it inspectable. Local expertise should remain connected to the person, place and circumstances that give it meaning. Research should retain the boundaries that determine how confidently it can inform a different setting.

Map the Outcome Insights · Essay 15

Good news needs the same scrutiny as bad news

Imagine two updates arriving on the same day. One reports encouraging uptake. The other suggests that a less visible group is struggling to participate. A useful review process should give both observations a fair...

aid transparencyAI evidence systemsprogramme design

Imagine two updates arriving on the same day. One reports encouraging uptake. The other suggests that a less visible group is struggling to participate. A useful review process should give both observations a fair opportunity to change the programme's understanding.

In experiments involving policymakers, practitioners and researchers, Vivalt and Coville found that participants updated more in response to favourable findings and were relatively insensitive to confidence intervals. They did not find significant differences in these biases between the professional groups. The study concerns its recruited participants, not every person making development decisions. Vivalt and Coville, 2023

The product implication I draw is that evidence review should deliberately make uncertainty and disagreement easy to find. A promising result deserves questions about its source, coverage and alternative explanations. A disappointing finding deserves the same care rather than immediate dismissal as an implementation anomaly.

For MtO, an outcome record should have space for supporting evidence, conflicting evidence and unresolved interpretation. Approval should establish what a source supports, not whether its message is welcome.

I would also preserve a short account of what changed the team's view. Did the new information alter a decision, reduce confidence or prompt further investigation? That history would make learning more concrete than a retrospective statement that the team remained flexible.

A credible programme story can contain progress and uncertainty at the same time. Giving both a place in the record is a practical standard for the system I want MtO to become.
